# ColorHugs — Content Design

What goes inside each universe, and what each thing rests on.

Status: `[PROPOSAL]` throughout. Nothing here is approved. It is written to be
argued with.

---

## How to read this

Every concept below is written as four things:

- **What the child does** — the actual interaction, not a category.
- **What it rests on** — the mechanism, named honestly.
- **Evidence** — one of three words, used strictly:
  - **Established** — a robust literature, replicated, uncontroversial.
  - **Reasonable** — supported, but narrower or noisier than the popular claim.
  - **Practice** — clinical or classroom tradition with thin experimental
    support. Legitimate to build; not legitimate to advertise as proven.
- **Build cost** — Small (content only), Medium (content + interaction),
  Large (needs a service: audio, AI, moderation, drawing capture).

No claim is made stronger than the evidence behind it. That constraint is the
product's main asset: a psychology-informed platform that overstates its
science is worth less than one that has none.

## Three flags before anything else

### 1. "Brain Gym" is a name with a problem

**Brain Gym®** is an existing trademarked educational programme (Dennison), and
it is one of the most frequently cited examples of pseudoscience in education —
widely criticised by educational psychologists and neuroscientists for claims
about movement rewiring the brain. Two consequences:

- **Credibility.** The one audience most likely to recognise the name is the
  one whose trust the product most needs: psychologists, teachers, SEN staff.
  For a platform whose differentiator is that a paediatric psychologist stands
  behind it, sharing a name with a discredited programme is an own goal.
- **Trademark.** It is a registered mark in several jurisdictions. Worth a
  legal check before launch, independent of the credibility question.

The artwork is beautiful and the concept is sound. It is the *name* that
carries the risk. **This needs a decision, and it is cheaper now than after the
brand is known.** Alternatives in the same spirit: *Think Gym*, *Mind Gym*,
*Focus Lab*, *Puzzle Lab*.

### 2. Cognitive training does not transfer the way people assume

Practising working-memory tasks reliably makes children better at *those tasks*.
The evidence that it improves attention, reasoning or school results in general
is weak and heavily contested — this is one of the better-replicated
disappointments in the field. So:

- Never claim ColorHugs makes a child's memory, focus or intelligence better.
- Frame these as games that are enjoyable, that build task-specific skill, and
  that teach **strategies** the child can name and reuse ("I said it out loud",
  "I made a picture in my head"). Strategy instruction has better support than
  raw drilling.
- If a parent asks what Brain Gym does, the honest answer is: it is playful
  practice with attention and memory, and children enjoy getting better at it.

### 3. Literacy and numeracy cannot be translated

Phonics is a property of a specific language. A Portuguese child learning
letter–sound correspondence and an English child learning it are doing
different tasks with different orders of difficulty and different exceptions.
Word Explorer in EN, FR, ES, PT-PT, PT-BR, ZH and HI is **seven designs**, not
one design translated seven times. ZH is not alphabetic at all.

Numeracy travels much better. Emotional content travels well but needs cultural
review, not just translation.

**Planning consequence:** launch literacy in one or two languages done properly,
rather than seven done badly.

## Learning Hub

*Read • Count • Discover*

### Word Explorer

**What the child does.** Hears a word, sees it broken into sounds, and builds
it from sound tiles — then meets the same word again minutes later, and again
days later, inside a picture or a short sentence.

**Rests on.** Phonemic awareness and systematic phonics for decoding;
spaced retrieval for vocabulary retention. **Evidence: established** for both,
and among the strongest findings in education research.

**Distinctive.** Most word apps show a word and ask the child to tap the right
picture — recognition, which is far easier than production and feels like
learning without being it. Building the word from sounds is production, and the
spacing schedule is invisible to the child.

**Build: Medium**, plus recorded or synthesised audio per language.

### Number Adventure

**What the child does.** Places a number on an unmarked line between two known
points; estimates rather than counts; then checks. Later, splits a quantity two
ways and sees both parts at once.

**Rests on.** Number-line estimation and part–whole understanding, which
predict later arithmetic better than counting fluency does.
**Evidence: reasonable to established** — number-line training has repeatedly
shown gains in magnitude understanding.

**Distinctive.** Almost every counting app drills arithmetic facts. Magnitude
and part–whole are what actually underpin them, and they are visual, which
suits a child who cannot yet read.

**Build: Medium.**

### Story Time

**What the child does.** Listens to a story read aloud, with the option to
follow the text; is stopped twice to predict what happens next; retells the
ending in their own words afterwards (spoken or drawn).

**Rests on.** Listening comprehension, which is half of reading ability and
develops years before decoding; prediction and retelling as comprehension
strategies. **Evidence: established.**

**Distinctive.** A read-aloud that asks the child to *do* something with the
story. And a retell captured as a drawing is something the child can keep — it
feeds My Sticker Book, so comprehension work produces an artefact rather than a
score.

**Build: Large** (audio, and capture of the retell).

### School Challenge

**What the child does.** Short sets tied to what is being covered at school
that term, chosen by the parent from a simple list.

**Rests on.** Retrieval practice and distributed practice.
**Evidence: established.**

**Honest limitation.** Curricula differ by country and by year. This is the
most content-hungry activity in the product and the least reusable across
markets. Consider making it the **last** thing built, not the first.

**Build: Large** (curriculum mapping per market).

## Brain Gym *(name under review — see flag 1)*

*Focus • Think • Solve*

### Focus Mission

**What the child does.** Watches for one thing and ignores others, with the
distractors getting more tempting rather than the targets getting faster.

**Rests on.** Selective attention and inhibitory control.
**Evidence: reasonable** for task-specific improvement; **do not claim**
improved attention at school.

**Distinctive.** Difficulty rises through *interference*, not speed. Speed-based
difficulty punishes slower processors and children with motor differences for
something unrelated to the skill.

**Build: Medium.**

### Memory Challenge

**What the child does.** Remembers a growing sequence — and, crucially, is
offered a strategy afterwards and asked whether it helped.

**Rests on.** Working memory capacity plus **strategy instruction** (rehearsal,
chunking, visual imagery). **Evidence: established for strategy instruction**,
weak for capacity training. The strategy half is the part that earns its place.

**Distinctive.** The game is the wrapper; the strategy is the content. A child
who leaves saying "I say them out loud to remember" has gained something a
scoreboard cannot give.

**Build: Medium.**

### Think & Solve

**What the child does.** Puzzles with more than one route to the answer, where
the child is asked *how* they got there before being told whether it is right.

**Rests on.** Metacognition and explanation. Self-explanation reliably improves
transfer. **Evidence: established.**

**Build: Medium**, plus a way to capture "how" without requiring writing —
picking from illustrated strategy cards, not free text.

### Speedy Brain

**What the child does.** Fast, easy decisions, for the pleasure of fluency.

**Rests on.** Processing speed and automaticity of already-learned material.
**Evidence: reasonable** — automaticity of *known* facts frees capacity;
speeding up unknown material just produces guessing.

**Design constraint.** Only ever use material the child has already mastered
elsewhere. A timer on something not yet learned is a stress machine.

**Build: Small.**

## My Inner World

*Discover • Grow • Shine*

This is the section that most distinguishes ColorHugs and the one where the
boundary is thinnest. Rule for all of it: **describe, never assess**. Nothing
here produces a score, a trend line, or anything resembling a screening result.

### How Do I Feel?

**Status: `[DEFINED]` and designed. See Decision Log increment 20.**

**What the child does.** Picks one of seven feeling cards. If she wants, she
points to where in her body she feels it, on a neutral outline figure. Then the
activity closes. A child who reads can tap again to open the family into three
or four finer words — *chateado*, *desiludido*, *com saudades*.

**What was removed from this proposal, and why.**
- **Intensity is out** (D-096). "How big is it" produces *my sadness is a 5*
  with nothing that follows, and turns a vocabulary tool into something shaped
  like a measure.
- **The body map is in, but the location is not stored** (D-105). The map is
  the moment; the card is the record. Storing both gives a two-dimensional
  record, which is the shape of a clinical instrument.
- **The fine layer is optional and comes after the activity has closed**
  (D-100), so the child who cannot read is not left with an unfinished
  activity.

**Rests on.** Affect labelling and emotion granularity. Naming an emotion
specifically is associated with reduced physiological arousal and better
regulation; children with finer emotion vocabulary regulate better.
**Evidence: reasonable**, and among the better-supported ideas in SEL.

**Distinctive.** Almost every child emotion app has five faces and stops there.
Granularity — the vocabulary *growing* as the child does — is the actual
mechanism, and nobody is building it for children.

**Serious design constraints, now decided.**
- **Nothing is visible to the parent** (D-098, closes Q-017). No summary, no
  chart, no alert. Stated out loud on the parents page, because a parent who
  finds nothing assumes it is broken.
- **A short trail, never an aggregate** (D-097). The child sees the last few
  cards she chose, in order. No counts, no percentages, no "your most frequent
  feeling" — that sentence has the shape of a diagnosis.
- No "correct" feeling, ever. **Free with no limit** (D-099): an activity for
  naming what you feel cannot cap how many times you may feel.
- Q-015 remains open but is narrower: with no intensity recorded, the product
  has no measure of severity, only of repetition. That is the right information
  not to have.

**Who gets what** (D-163). Declared in `src/data/access.ts`; not enforced until
Q-010 is answered.

| Family — free | yes | — | — |
| Family — premium | yes | yes | — |
| Professional — education | yes | yes | — |
| Professional — health | yes | yes | yes |

Naming what you feel is never sold, nor is the audio, nor the first strategies
of any feeling (D-142).

**Three settings**: the interactive activity, a seven-card printable deck with
a secondary weekly sheet (D-108), and a professional workbook (D-144) at
`docs/materials/zangado-caderno.md`.

**Build: Medium.**

### Calm My Body

**What the child does.** Follows a paced breath with a visual and a sound —
breathing out longer than in — and afterwards says whether their body feels
different.

**Rests on.** Paced breathing with extended exhalation shifts autonomic
arousal. **Evidence: established for acute arousal reduction**; not a treatment
for anxiety, and must never be described as one.

**Distinctive.** Add progressive muscle relaxation in a child-sized form
("squeeze like a lemon, let go") and a "which one worked for you?" at the end.
The child builds a small personal toolkit rather than being handed one
technique.

**Build: Small to Medium** (audio helps a lot).

### My Worries

**What the child does.** Draws or names a worry, decides whether it is
something they can do something about, and either makes a plan or puts it in
the worry box until worry time.

**Rests on.** Worry postponement and the controllable/uncontrollable split, from
CBT. **Evidence: reasonable in adults, practice in children** — say so plainly.

**Design constraint.** Worries are child-authored free text or drawings, so
this is the highest-risk input surface in the whole product. Anything a child
writes here could be a disclosure. **Requires a decided, clinically reviewed
protocol before a single line is built** — including what is never stored.

**Build: Large** (moderation, storage decisions, protocol).

### My Superpowers

**What the child does.** Notices a strength they used this week, picked from
illustrated cards, and says when they used it — or a parent nominates one they
saw.

**Rests on.** Character-strengths spotting and strengths-based feedback.
**Evidence: reasonable.**

**Distinctive.** The parent-nomination route. A child being told by their own
adult "I saw you being brave on Tuesday" is worth more than any badge the
software can issue, and it is the one place where bringing the parent *in* to
the child's screen improves things rather than intruding.

**Build: Small.**

## Kids Draw for Kids

*Drawn by Kids • Colored by Kids*

The six mission levels are already approved and fun-first. What is worth adding
is what happens *around* the drawing.

### The strongest idea in the product

**A child's drawing becomes a colouring page for other children.** A submitted
drawing, once reviewed, is converted to clean outlines and enters the Color &
Create library credited to a pseudonym.

Why it matters: it closes a loop nothing else on the market closes. The child
is not just praised, they are *useful* — another child somewhere colours their
dragon. That is a genuinely different emotional proposition from a gallery.

**Rests on.** Purpose and contribution as motivators; authorship.
**Evidence: reasonable** for autonomy and purpose in motivation research.

**Build: Large** — needs image processing, human review, and a clear
IP/consent position on reuse of child-created work. **The consent question is
not trivial and must be settled with legal advice.**

### Listen & Draw, revisited

**What the child does.** Draws only from spoken instruction, with no picture to
copy — then compares with what other children drew from the same words.

**Rests on.** Listening comprehension and mental imagery.
**Evidence: established** for listening comprehension.

**Distinctive.** The comparison at the end. Twenty children drew twenty
different things from identical words, and none is wrong. That is a lesson
about interpretation that no worksheet delivers.

**Build: Medium**, plus review of every drawing shown.

## Color & Create

*Draw • Color • Imagine*

### Explore & Color

Free in full. The interesting design question is **sorting**, not content: a
pre-reader cannot use a text menu. Sort visually — by what is in the picture,
not by category name.

**Build: Small.** Add printable output; paper still matters, and it is the one
feature that gets a child *off* the screen. Worth treating as a feature, not an
afterthought.

### Imagine & Create

**What the child does.** Describes an idea and gets a colouring page of it —
but is scaffolded into describing well: what is it, where is it, what is it
doing, what else is there.

**Rests on.** Expressive language, elaboration and sequencing. The child has to
turn a mental image into words precise enough to be understood.
**Evidence: established** for the link between elaborated expression and
language development.

**Distinctive, and this is the point worth protecting.** Framed as an art tool
it is a novelty. Framed as a **language activity disguised as art**, it becomes
the most defensible AI feature in any children's product — the AI is not doing
the child's creative work, it is giving them a reason to describe something
precisely and an immediate reward for doing it well.

**Build: Large.** Input moderation, output moderation, cost per use, and the
key must live on a server. Already Premium and already exempt from sampling.

## My ColorHugs

*Small Steps, Big Achievements!*

### My Progress

**Design principle:** the child sees what they **did**, never what they got
wrong. Activities completed, drawings made, days they showed up — a record, not
an assessment.

**Rests on.** Effort and mastery orientation. Praise for effort and strategy
rather than ability supports persistence.
**Evidence: reasonable** — the effect is real but smaller and more conditional
than the popular version suggests.

### Next Goal

**What the child does.** Picks their own next small goal from two or three
offered.

**Rests on.** Proximal, specific, self-chosen goals outperform distant or
imposed ones. **Evidence: established.**

**Distinctive.** The child chooses. One line of design, and it converts a
to-do list into agency.

### My Sticker Book and Trophy Shelf

Collection as its own reward, permanent by rule. Worth deciding: stickers earned
by **participation and variety** rather than performance — a child who tried
all seven areas has done something worth marking.

**Build: Small to Medium.**

## Community

*Together we share, together we grow*

Community Favorite already exists as recognition rather than competition. Two
additions in the same spirit:

- **A weekly whole-community drawing prompt**, where every submission is shown
  together as one wall. Belonging without ranking, and it produces a genuinely
  striking artefact each week — three hundred children's versions of the same
  idea.
- **Kindness marks instead of likes.** Fixed, positive, non-numeric reactions
  that the receiving child sees as a total but never as a comparison. If a
  count can be compared, it becomes a leaderboard whatever it is called.

**Build: Medium**, entirely dependent on the moderation pipeline.

## What runs across everything

**Audio-first.** A four-year-old cannot read the instructions. Every
instruction, mission and label spoken as well as written is the single change
that most widens who can use ColorHugs alone — and it is the accessibility
feature that also happens to be a market advantage. **Build: Large**, and it
multiplies by the number of languages.

**No timers where feeling is involved.** Timers belong in Speedy Brain and
nowhere in My Inner World.

**Every activity ends with something kept, not scored.** A drawing, a sticker,
a strategy the child can name. The artefact is the reward; there is no number.

**The free sample is the first three items, everywhere.** Already decided
(D-037). It means every activity must be designed as an ordered set with a
sensible first three — which is a useful discipline, not a constraint.

## What I would build first, and why

**Calm My Body.** Small, self-contained, needs no AI, no moderation, no content
library and no child-authored input. It is the fastest route to a real,
finished, end-to-end activity — and finishing one teaches more about the other
thirty than sketching thirty ever will.

**Then How Do I Feel?**, because it is the section that most distinguishes
ColorHugs and the one whose design constraints most need working out early.

**Not first:** anything needing moderation, AI, curriculum mapping, or
child-authored text. Those are correct to build; they are wrong to build before
there is one finished activity to learn from.
